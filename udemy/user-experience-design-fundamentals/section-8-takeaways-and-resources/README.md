# Section 8 — Takeaways and Resources

Summary of lectures 46–53. The closing section: a set of parting thoughts on what user experience
actually is, a catalogue of the **UX tasks and techniques** used on projects (with what each is good
and bad for), and then a tour of the **software and websites** the instructor recommends —
wireframing/prototyping, A/B testing, content inventory, user testing, analytics, and educational
reading.

> **User experience equals value.** If there's no value to users, there is no value coming back to
> you or to the client you're working for.

## 46. Things to Remember

UX design encompasses **a whole lot more than what's seen on the screen** — more than what the
system does, what it looks like, or how it acts. It's informed by, and derived from, the
**attitudes, expectations, and preconceptions** we all carry about how a thing is supposed to work,
how we envision it will work, how we speculate it might work.

### Incentives, value, and trust

- Whether an experience is positive or negative depends largely on the **incentives** people have
  to use the thing. That scenario gave birth to the term **change management** in B2B
  environments: to make people use something, you first have to persuade them to change their
  behavior.
- You do that by satisfying their needs, giving them a good reason to participate — in other words,
  by **providing value**. Good UX "looks like, feels like, smells like, and is by its very nature"
  value.
- The value has to exist **for the end users and for the creator** (you, or the business you work
  for).
- There has to be **trust**, built by enabling **successful task completion** — fast, intuitive,
  efficient. When any one of those fails, the thing falls flat on its face.

### The user's mantra

> **If this doesn't make my life easier, I'm not using it.**

We all live in information overload daily, so anything that doesn't simplify a work or personal
process is **ignored or misused**. If a screen of any kind is your delivery medium, the user
experience is **the critical indicator of the value you're providing**.

## 47. Tools of the Trade

The tasks and activities that occur during a project. Different tools suit different project types
and budget sizes — but **there's almost always some form of user research you can do, even on the
tiniest budget**.

A recurring theme ("I sense a theme here"): many of these are **not good for projects with very
tight deadlines and small budgets**.

| Technique | What it is | Good for | Not so good for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Analytics** | Evaluating quantitative data to understand what people do with a product or service. | Identifying interesting behavior (is it the norm? did it change after you changed something?); focusing research on *why* people behave that way. | Tight deadlines/small budgets; clients with no analytics data (a site up three years with nothing tracked); explaining the complete reason behind behavior. |
| **Competitor benchmarking** | Evaluating competing products for strengths and weaknesses — which reveals opportunities to innovate. | Crowded spaces with lots to observe; immersing yourself in an unfamiliar client industry and its customs. | Tight schedules; sectors you already know intimately (spend the time on user research instead). |
| **Contextual research** | Researching in the user's typical environment — sitting next to them, shadowing, watching what they do and how they react. One of the most effective UX activities. | The most accurate insight into problems people face and how they react in their own environment; uncovering the workarounds they currently use. | Tight deadlines and small budgets — clients aren't always willing to pay for it. |
| **Experience maps** | Visualizations of the process people follow before, during, and after using a product or service. | Visualizing the entire customer journey and where it shines or fails; keeping task steps to a minimum; making key tasks easy. | Single-step tasks; tight deadlines/small budgets. |
| **Guerrilla usability testing** | Very informal user involvement when little or no testing budget exists. | Involving users when budgets can't support large-scale research; quick opinions to evolve design concepts. | Recruiting for a very specific scenario (it's generalized and not detailed); situations where the client needs to observe — you'll do more work presenting and convincing. |
| **Expert reviews** | An expert evaluates a product against usability guidelines related to target users and tasks. | A quick understanding of key usability issues — quick and dirty, "I've seen this before." | A deep understanding of real user issues — it's gut reaction and pattern recognition. |
| **Ideation workshop** | Collaborative method where you and the client decide on design solutions together. | A shared vision; early input from visual designers, developers, and other disciplines — "all your oars rowing in the same direction." | Clients who don't want to be involved and would rather you "work your magic" (which isn't really possible). |
| **Information architecture** | Organizing information so content relationships are obvious and retrieval is simple. | Structure and navigation reflecting specific user and business needs. **Essential on every project — not optional.** | Small projects like emails and campaign landing pages with little information to organize. |
| **Lab usability testing** | End users test the product in a controlled lab (20–40+ recorded, monitored desks). | Letting clients observe in real time; testing the right people with controlled interruptions. | Tight deadlines/small budgets; **environment-specific products** — a quiet, comfortable lab won't reproduce a noisy, hot, dirty factory. |
| **Personas** | Short, vivid descriptions of fictional characters representing users. | Giving teams an easy way to understand needs and core tasks; collaborative persona-building exercises ("why are we designing this feature this way?"). | Situations with no research on real users to base them on; personas generated externally and never shared with the client, developers, and everyone else — then they're useless. |
| **Prototyping** | Quickly mocking up ideas in interactive form to elicit feedback. | A shared understanding and **approval of direction** (can't be overemphasized); a candidate design for user testing — test, revise, repeat. | Tight deadlines/small budgets. |
| **Requirements workshops** | Gathering key stakeholders to discuss the product's use through exercises. | Complex design challenges, complex clients, and **disparate teams** (split by discipline, geography, etc.). | Tight deadlines/small budgets — requires a tremendous amount of time and involvement. |
| **Stakeholder interviews** | Interviews with key individuals in the client's organization to understand business requirements. | Learning the business goals and what people think success is; clients with many invested people and departments. | Short turnarounds (interviewing, transcribing, analyzing takes time); making technical, functional, or visual decisions — everyone's take is subjective and colored by their position. |
| **Surveys** | A predetermined, identical set of questions sent to a geographically scattered group. | Reaching scattered people in a short time; collecting both qualitative and quantitative data. | High data accuracy (you need very many participants to allow for error, subjectivity, and people not telling the whole truth); quick analysis — qualitative analysis is extremely time-consuming. |
| **Task models** | Descriptions of the activities users perform to reach their goals. | Matching the product to expectations; insight into a **buying process** to design transactional experiences. | Tight deadlines/small budgets — detailed workflow analysis takes a lot of time. |
| **Wireframing** | Static diagrams of a product's framework, exploring content, navigation, and interactions. | Exploring ideas cheaply before visual design and development; multiple concrete options to evaluate; testing with users and refining; **agreeing direction with a client** without them getting hung up on whether they like orange. | Highly interactive products that must be used to be understood — where physical use matters as much as the screen, a wireframe gives only half the picture. |

> Analytics are great, but they **only tell you part of the story** behind why people do what they
> do — environment, daily pressures, and natural inclinations are unknowns.

## 48. Wireframing and Prototyping Software

More tools keep cropping up and existing ones keep getting better. There are **subtle
differences** between them; you may use one or another (or both) depending on what you have to do,
who it's for, and what the outcome needs to be.

| Tool | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Balsamiq Mockups** | Rapid wireframing that reproduces the experience of sketching on a whiteboard, but easier to share and modify. | The easiest of the bunch — very low learning curve. Wireframes **look like sketches**, so stakeholders focus on content, features, and functions instead of visual details. The recommended starting point if you're new to wireframing (balsamiq.com). |
| **Axure** | Drag-and-drop detailed wireframes with boxes, placeholders, shapes, and text. | The instructor's personal favorite. **Masters** create reusable elements (headers, footers, page templates) — change one and it updates everywhere. One click generates an **HTML/JavaScript prototype** viewable in IE, Firefox, Safari, and Chrome; publish to a network drive, web server, or Axure's own hosting service so others can interact without installing anything. |
| **HotGloo** | Wireframing and prototyping in one tool. | Simple interface makes selecting and placing objects easy; teams can wireframe together in real time. Easy linking of elements to pages and setting up user scenarios with states and view stacks. Has a **built-in chat** for clients and coworkers — probably unique among these tools. |
| **UXPin** | A complete toolkit of built-in design elements and patterns. | Like Balsamiq, good for people new to UX. Makes **responsive design** easy with built-in breakpoints for multiple devices and resolutions. Built-in collaboration with teammates, clients, and stakeholders. |

The more collaboration in a design process, the better off you are — so tools that encourage it
bring real benefits. There are many more, but **these four are the place to start**.

## 49. A/B Testing Software

**A/B testing** means testing two versions of the same page or screen:

- **Version A** — the original control page, the baseline, also called the **champion** (like the
  reigning heavyweight champion).
- **Version B** — an alternative layout, the **challenger**.

Put both out there, measure user behavior (what people do, look at, click on), and see which
performs better. If the challenger wins, it **replaces the champion** and becomes the one to beat
in later tests. The idea is **constant improvement**.

| Tool | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Optimizely** | Tracks engagement, clicks, conversions, signups — anything that matters to the business. | Built-in **custom goal tracking**: you define what to measure, it does the rest. When a variation wins you can automatically send 100% of visitors to it, buying time to deploy changes to the live site. Unlike Google Website Optimizer, it needs only **one line of code per page**, not code per experiment — a big deal on large sites. |
| **Visual Website Optimizer** | Tests page variations and tracks conversions to find the best performer. | **No HTML experience required.** Point it at a URL, it grabs the page, and you create variations on the fly. **Tagless integration** means little dependence on IT or developers. Ideal for people without advanced technical skills. |

## 50. Content Inventory Software

A **content inventory** is the foundation of just about any redesign: a **quantitative look at all
the content** people will engage with — typically a list of content files used in a site or app,
and the starting point on the path from the **as-is** state to the **to-be** state.

### Why create one

- Assess the **as-is landscape** of the site, app, or system.
- **Accurately estimate** the amount of work, based on how much content you'll deal with.
- Identify **patterns in content structure**, especially in existing infrastructure.
- Set a **baseline** to measure where you started versus where you end up.
- **Track migration** — if you'll take only 50% of current content: what did we keep, throw out,
  revise?

The time to do it is **at the start of any design or redesign project**, and any time you migrate
or upgrade a CMS or migrate content in a redesign.

Inventory software gives data on each page — links in and out, images, videos, documents, and
metadata. **Comparison features** matter too: run and save an inventory, run it again later, and
quickly locate new, removed, or changed files. That supports efficient quality assurance and a clear
picture at any given time — **understanding where you are makes it easier to plan where you want to
be.**

| Tool | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Content Analysis Tool (CAT)** by Content Insight | Automates building a comprehensive content inventory in one tool. | "The best and brightest" and by far the most comprehensive. Communicates site size to stakeholders and creates base documents for content audits. Captures URLs, file types, images, videos, documents, metadata, text, links in/out — plus a **screenshot of each page**, a visual reference for when you're knee-deep at the 11th hour. |
| **Screaming Frog** | Spiders a site's links, images, CSS, scripts, and apps; presents key page elements in tabs by type, filterable for common SEO issues. | Not built for content inventory, but serves the purpose very well. Good for medium-to-large sites where manual checking is too labor-intensive. Its depth catches redirects, meta refreshes, and duplicate-page issues other inventory tools may miss. |
| **Xenu's Link Sleuth** | A no-frills broken-link checker; a comprehensive inventory is a byproduct. | Verifies normal links, images, frames, plug-ins, backgrounds, local image maps, stylesheets, scripts, Java applets. Produces a continuously updated, sortable URL list and reports. Dead simple, yet does this job better than a lot of polished software. **Free.** |

> Better to err on the side of **too much information** about content than too little. If you miss
> something, it's really painful to wedge it in later when you've already built a foundation — those
> "blind corners" become real problems in development.

## 51. User Testing and Feedback Software

User testing is becoming an important part of any design (especially redesign) process: finding out
what people will and won't use, what they prefer, and **the difference between what they say they
do and what they actually do**.

The internet makes it easy to get direct feedback from real users with a quick, cheap,
**guerrilla-style** approach. With a phone and a computer you can do **remote user research** — you
don't have to be in the room, and results can be just as useful. You can talk to people face to face
or let an online tool gather the information automatically.

| Tool | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Silverback** | Records the screen and screen activity (mouse clicks) plus video of the participant's face and voice. | Add **chapter markers** to label what the person was doing; export to QuickTime and share. Uses the Mac's built-in hardware — no setup, no expense — but it's **Mac only**. Virtually no learning curve; built with designers and developers in mind. |
| **Loop11** | Online remote usability testing: an online survey mixing questions with tasks on a website. | Set a specific goal per task and measure whether users achieve it or how hard it was. Tests are **unmoderated** — design, publish, send a link, and people complete it at home at their own pace (no wrangling schedules). Main advantage: **an unlimited number of users** — the more the merrier, the more accurate the results. |
| **Treejack** | Tests information architectures with a clickable tree structure. | Participants get a task (e.g. *find acne treatment cream*), expand branches, and click **"I'd find it here."** Results show categories chosen, percentage of correct choices, and paths taken — revealing content buried too deep, confusing labels, or wrong priorities. The instructor has yet to find a project that didn't benefit immensely. |
| **UserTesting.com** | Simple, intuitive analysis of a website at a fraction of competitors' cost. | See real-time interaction, **hear what people think** as they use the site, and read a written summary. A database of **paid remote users** matching your demographic does the legwork of recruiting. Produces qualitative rather than statistical data — but with high participant volume, commonalities emerge (30 people hitting the same thing is a common theme, good or bad). |

## 52. Analytics Software

Whether you're building a website, an app, or a system, you're trying to **scratch an itch** someone
has. Knowing exactly how to serve them is key to getting them to take action, and analytics help
maximize performance and bottom-line results.

At its most basic, analytics measures **traffic and flow**: how many people come, how many interact,
what they do, where they go from A to B to C, how long they stay, and where they exit.

Why care? **The only reason your site or app exists is to prompt someone to take a particular
action** — a purchase, a click, reading an article, referring a friend. You need to know whether
you're meeting that goal, and if you have to change your game plan, **you don't want to guess at
these answers**.

### What to look for in any solution

- The ability to **categorize collected information** any way you see fit.
- A high degree of **abstraction**, so you focus on the important stuff instead of fighting clutter
  and minute details that don't help you make decisions.
- Information **specifically tailored** to what you're doing — everything else is a waste of time.

### The big three

| Tool | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Google Analytics** | Free web analytics packed with many of the same reports as high-priced solutions. | "The reigning heavyweight champion since 2006." Drill-down reports identify **high-exit pages** so you can repurpose content driving people away; shows traffic patterns for new or updated content and which topics attract the most attention. Most bang for the buck, great slicing and dicing — **the best place to start**. |
| **Clicky** | A dashboard of core data: visitors (today vs. yesterday or any range), actions, bounce rate, top content, top search terms, traffic sources. | At-a-glance reporting up front, with deeper segmenting and filtering (best-converting traffic sources, pages grabbing or losing attention). Has the power of Google but better **progressive disclosure** — gives you what you need now without forcing the rest on you. |
| **Woopra** | Integrates site analytics with **customer relationship management**, tracking individual visitors across multiple devices. | A holistic approach; attractive and highly customizable — use it online, via desktop client, or mobile app. Customization (endless custom filters) is its strongest point. Unique **live chat** feature invites visitors to chat when criteria are met (e.g. after checkout), giving feedback beyond the numbers — people prefer talking to a live human over filling out a boring survey. |

## 53. Educational and Informational Websites

Information on great UX — advice, how-tos, step-by-step instructions, video tutorials — is all out
there, and a good set of sites can in and of itself give you a decent education.

This is a **deceptive profession: the deeper you get into it, the more there is to know**, partly
because we're dealing with human beings, who are complex creatures — and technology keeps adding
new tools, techniques, plug-ins, and platforms. It's **constant evolution**; to avoid getting left
behind, you have to stay on top of it and **widen your palette** as challenges grow more complex.

The instructor's four daily sites:

| Site | What it is | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **UX Booth** | A publication by and for the UX community; readership mostly beginning-to-intermediate UX and interaction designers, but open to anyone making the web better. | A collective of authors and contributors, mostly practicing professionals, grown into a big community. A diverse, sizable range of topics — a good mix of traditional and non-traditional thinking. |
| **A List Apart** | Insightful articles on the design, development, and meaning of web content, with a focus on **web standards and best practices**. | Hard to beat for quality and integrity. Written by experienced designers, developers, architects, producers, and project managers. Topics range from code, content, and culture to design, mobile, process, user science, and philosophy. |
| **UX Magazine** | Fairly new, but quickly became a **central one-stop resource** for everything UX, with a steady stream of current, credible information. | Content from an impressive roster of experienced professionals across UX. Fosters communication between people who must collaborate well — designers and engineers, clients and vendors, designers and business strategists. |
| **Smashing Magazine** | One of the most popular resources for UX and UI design — usability, IA, interaction design, and more. | Publishes **"best of" lists** with visual examples (e.g. ten sites with excellent checkout processes, ten apps with innovative navigation). Covers physical products too — signage, wayfinding. Experts share tips, guidelines, best practices, and case studies. |

> I never want just one position, one point of view, one frame of mind — even my own. It's
> infinitely more valuable to get a **diverse collection of thoughts, opinions, and perspectives**,
> then make up your own mind based on those and on your experience applying them.

### Give Good UX and *Think First*

The instructor's own site, **givegoodux.com**, offers articles, videos, tutorials, and practical
advice across UX — career guidance, UI design, customer experience — drawn from three decades of
working with Fortune 500 and 100 companies. It also has free ebooks, UX templates, information on
online courses, and UX training and consulting services for individuals and organizations.

It's also where to find his book, **_Think First_** — a roadmap to creating solid UX strategy:

> Good user experience **starts between your ears**. What makes your work most valuable is **how you
> think about the decisions you make.**

Most resources offer tactical how-to advice but gloss over the thinking part: understanding what
people want from you and **solving the right problems** instead of throwing time and activity at
everything that comes down the pike. Anything used by people needs a solid understanding of its
value and of the features, functions, and tasks that contribute to it. The book is a step-by-step
guide to producing something **useful, usable, and valuable**.

The course closes with an invitation to get in touch and a reminder to always **give good UX**.
