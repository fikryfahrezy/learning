# Lesson 3: Decision-making AI

## What is decision-making AI?

Decision-making AI turns data and goals into action. When there are several possible paths, it evaluates trade-offs, follows constraints and rules, and recommends the next step. In some cases, decision-making AI runs predefined responses when speed matters. Unlike predictive AI, which estimates what might happen, decision-making AI chooses actions. It uses predictions and other inputs to align choices with ideal outcomes. Common domains include finance, healthcare, transportation, and operations.

> **Decision-making AI** evaluates multiple possible actions and chooses the optimal action based on factors including efficiency, accuracy, and suggested outcomes.

In practice, decision-making AI works in three ways:  

1. It selects the best action such as surfacing a single, well-matched recommendation.  
2. It optimizes outcomes by allocating limited time, cost, or capacity for the greatest benefit.  
3. It automates decisions such as instantly approving, routing, or blocking according to set policies.

The outputs might include ranked options with rationales, optimized schedules or allocations, or automatic pass-or-fail decisions. These outputs are always bounded by human-defined objectives and guardrails. People retain the ability to review, approve, or override the decision-making AI’s outputs.

## Key differences between predictive AI and decision-making AI

Imagine running a busy store where every tool has a different job. One tool helps you predict how many customers will visit next week, while another enables you to plan the fastest delivery routes. This is how different types of AI systems work in business and everyday life. Each has a unique function, from forecasting trends to making informed decisions that enhance efficiency.

### Predictive AI

**Predictive AI** focuses on making informed predictions about what will happen next, based on patterns it identifies in data. It answers the question “What will happen next?” or “How much should we expect?” It doesn’t create new things or make decisions. It predicts. You provide large amounts of historical data and patterns, and it delivers forecasts with confidence levels and probability scores about future outcomes.

### Decision-making AI

**Decision-making AI** is all about weighing choices and recommending the best one. It answers the question “Which option should you choose?” or “What’s the best way to do this?” It doesn’t make anything new, and it’s not about predicting the future. It decides. You provide available options and success criteria, and it ranks your choices while explaining the reasoning behind each recommendation.

## The role of decision-making AI in the AI ecosystem

Decision-making AI focuses on selecting the best action from multiple options, rather than just forecasting future events. While predictive AI analyzes data to determine what might happen next, decision-making AI uses those determinations to choose specific actions that achieve desired outcomes. This technology uses optimization algorithms to evaluate various possibilities and make informed decisions. Decision-making AI plays a crucial role in environments where action, not just insight, is required. Understanding this distinction helps clarify how AI tools support both planning and execution.

Decision-making AI is designed to support people, not replace them. These systems provide users with better tools and information, enabling them to make more informed and confident decisions while maintaining human control over the final choices.

For example, a supply chain system might use predictive AI to estimate future demand. Yet, it still turns to decision-making AI to assign inventory and schedule deliveries based on those forecasts. People can review, approve, or adjust recommendations in each step as needed.

### Selecting the best action

Decision-making AI compares all available options to identify the one that best aligns with the goals or rules set by users or organizations. It considers factors such as preferences, current conditions, and objectives. You’ll find this in everything from recommendation systems to self-driving cars.

For example, a streaming service uses decision-making AI to suggest movies that closely match a user’s past viewing habits and preferences.

### Optimizing outcomes

Decision-making AI excels in situations where resources, costs, or time must be carefully allocated and balanced to achieve optimal outcomes. By analyzing large amounts of data and using mathematical tools, decision-making AI identifies the solution that yields the best possible outcome. In fields such as logistics, finance, and energy, even minor improvements can yield substantial savings in terms of both financial resources and environmental impact.


For example, an energy provider uses decision-making AI to distribute electricity efficiently across the grid, facilitating a balance between supply, demand, and costs.

### Automating decisions

Decision-making AI can also make fast and reliable choices independently, without waiting for a person to intervene. It processes new information as it arrives and instantly acts according to established rules. This capability is valuable in tasks such as fraud detection, quality checks, and content moderation, where systems need to respond quickly and consistently.

For example, an online payment system uses decision-making AI to instantly block suspicious transactions, addressing users’ safety in real-time.

## AI ethics implications: Decision-making AI

Organizations use decision-making AI tools in various areas, including agriculture, manufacturing, and transportation. These tools can make processes more data-driven, accurate, and reliable, but their use also raises important questions about AI ethics.

Imagine a regional agricultural management team adopting decision-making AI to help manage agricultural operations such as water sensor allocation and usage, crop inputs including seed, fertilizer, crop protection, and soil treatment, and inspections across farms. Because the use of these tools can impact farmers’ livelihoods, safety, and production outcomes, the teams must consider the Pillars of Trust to help identify risks, foster trust, and align decisions with both organizational goals and stakeholder needs.

*Select each section to learn more about the AI ethics implications associated with decision-making AI.*

### Fairness

Regarding **fairness**, the teams verify that the AI systems don’t mistreat any group or repeat past patterns of discrimination. They compare outcomes across co-op groups, geographies, and suppliers to identify signs of bias so that similar cases receive similar decisions.

For example, the agricultural system should not favor farms from certain co-ops for seeding allocations over others.

### Robustness

The team verifies that the system is **robust** and **reliable**, regardless of attempts to compromise it or the presence of unexpected, poor-quality data. They test the AI with unusual cases and simulate sensor failures and intrusions to harden systems against manipulation that could lead to unsafe or unfair outcomes.

For example, the team works to maintain that farms with the same risk profile aren’t treated differently because of location or size.

### Explainability

To promote **explainability**, the team examines whether the AI system’s decisions are easily understandable and mapped to guidelines and evidence, so everyone can understand why particular actions or measures were performed or taken.

For example, suppose a particular farm location was rejected for an irrigation allocation. In that case, the team needs to provide a simple, understandable answer, such as which field metrics led to the decision.

### Transparency

**Transparency** requires clear information about data sources, data use, and how the system makes decisions, so that stakeholders can understand what’s happening behind the scenes. Decision-making AI systems often require more thorough transparency efforts than predictive systems because their outputs directly shape real-world operations.

For example, the team should share accessible explanations and artifacts, such as data origin and audit metrics, to inform farm-level decisions with stakeholders.

### Privacy

The team safeguards stakeholders’ **privacy**, including sensitive business or personal information, through limitations, access controls, and secure processing. Similar to transparency efforts, decision-making systems often warrant increased privacy protections because of the potential impact on stakeholders. Stakeholders should be informed about what data is being used and provided with some control over whether that information is included in the decision-making process.

For example, the team should limit the use of farm-identifiable records in agriculture to only that information which is necessary for operations.
