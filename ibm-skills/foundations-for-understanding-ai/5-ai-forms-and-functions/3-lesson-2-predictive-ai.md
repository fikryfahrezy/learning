## What is predictive AI?

Predictive AI uses patterns in historical and sometimes real-time data to estimate what is likely to happen next. It turns past behavior into insights and recommendations. For example, it can forecast product demand, estimate how long jobs will take, identify parts you might need, determine when customers are likely to leave, or warn about equipment failures. With these insights, teams can act proactively and reduce uncertainty.

> **Predictive AI** involves using statistical analysis and machine learning to identify patterns, anticipate behaviors, and forecast upcoming events.

Predictive AI powers three common roles: offering insights and providing recommendations, analyzing risk, and planning and forecasting. In practice, it surfaces likely next-best actions such as identifying which customers to engage. It estimates the probability and impact of adverse events including delays or defaults. It also projects future needs such as inventory, staffing, or maintenance windows. Its outputs are estimates, including scores, probabilities, or ranges, that are shaped by the quality and relevance of the underlying data. Predictive AI complements human judgment and other forms of AI, such as generative AI used for creating content and computer vision used for interpreting images.

## The role of predictive AI in the AI ecosystem

Predictive AI helps humans make informed predictions about what might happen next, using patterns identified in past data. This technology benefits planning, reduces risks, and enables better decisions across various fields.

*Select each section to learn more about the role of predictive AI in the AI ecosystem.*

### Offering insights and making recommendations

Predictive AI analyzes data to **offer insights and make recommendations**. This ability is similar to noticing that people always buy more ice cream when it’s hot outside and using that pattern to predict sales next summer.

For example, a retail manager uses predictive AI to identify trends so that the store has popular products in stock before demand increases.

### Analyzing risk

**Analyzing risk** involves determining what might go wrong and assessing the likelihood of it occurring. Predictive AI achieves this by analyzing past events and current trends to identify potential risks.

For example, a bank uses predictive AI to assess the likelihood of a loan applicant defaulting, based on their financial history and market data.

### Planning and forecasting

**Planning and forecasting** involve using data to inform future decisions, whether it’s for next week or next year. Predictive AI analyzes past events and helps leaders make informed decisions about budgets, staffing, or supplies.

For example, a logistics company uses predictive AI to forecast delivery times, enabling customers to anticipate when they can expect their packages.

## AI ethics implications: Predictive AI

Businesses and organizations use predictive AI in many areas, including hiring, lending, and criminal justice. Predictive AI can make processes faster and more consistent, but its use also raises AI ethics implications.

Imagine a team at a bank is developing a new AI system to help with loan applications. They’re using a predictive AI tool, which analyzes data to estimate an applicant’s ability to repay a loan. Because this tool will impact real customers’ financial opportunities, the team recognizes that promoting the responsible use of AI is vital for establishing trust and transparency. By observing the five Pillars of Trust, the team can identify potential issues, build trust with customers, and align with the bank’s objectives and the applicants’ needs.

*Select each section to learn more about the AI ethics implications associated with predictive AI.*

### Fairness

One of the team’s concerns is promoting **fairness**, so they verify that the AI system doesn’t mistreat any group or repeat past patterns of discrimination, and compare decisions across different applicant groups to identify signs of bias.

For example, the team must investigate and address the issue if the system is more likely to interpret a person from a specific background as a higher risk.

### Robustness

The team verifies that the system is **robust** and **reliable**, regardless of attempts to compromise it or the presence of unexpected, poor-quality data. The team tests the AI with unusual cases and searches for weak spots that might lead to errors or indicate someone is at a higher risk of default than they should be.

For example, the team strives to ensure that the system only uses relevant information about the customer and can tolerate minor errors in data.

### Explainability

To promote **explainability**, the team examines whether the AI system’s analysis can be easily understood if a customer is considered a higher risk. If someone asks, “Why do you think I’m a high risk?” the team wants the AI to provide a simple, understandable answer.

For example, if the AI determines someone is unlikely to repay a loan because they’ve defaulted on three loans in the past five years, the team should be prepared to provide that reasoning.

### Transparency

**Transparency** means the team documents how the AI works and maintains the process in a way that is open for review. Because predictive AI forecasts outcomes from patterns in data, transparency should cover data sources, model purpose, key features, known limitations, and performance across groups.

For example, the team should provide clear documentation to support accountability in how predictions are applied.

### Privacy

The team also safeguards the **privacy** and personal information of customers. They collect only necessary data and protect it with strong security. They also keep customers informed about the data being used and provide them with some control over their information.

For example, applicants should be provided with information about their data upon request, and corrections should be made when something is verified as incorrect.
