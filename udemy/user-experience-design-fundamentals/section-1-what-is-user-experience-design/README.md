## 1. Introduction: A UXD Parable

**The story.** Three blind men meet an elephant. One feels the legs and says it's like two branchless
trees; one feels the tail and says it's like a straw fan; one feels the trunk and says it's like a
snake. They argue forever and never agree, because none of them examined the whole animal.

> "How can anyone describe the whole until he has learned the total of the parts?"

**The lesson.** Developers, designers, and business people each touch only a small part of user
experience, so they walk away with a narrow, fragmented understanding of what it means.

Two things make this worse:

- Many UX practitioners make the discipline hard to explore from multiple perspectives — what we hear
  is often a limited set of pre-determined questions and answers.
- The discipline is genuinely complex, because its core subject is human beings.

Without examining multiple perspectives at once, the relationships that actually create a memorable
experience stay hidden. Hence this course: go back to the fundamentals.

## 2. What UXD *Isn't*

Framed around Whitney Hess's article on common myths and misconceptions.

**Historical note.** Before the web, this was just called *design*. Even print design was already
about people — what they react to, how they interpret things, what they want, need, and are willing
to use. Those same foundations sit under branding, marketing, advertising, and good design in general.

### Myth 1 — UXD is user interface design

UI is **one facet** of UX, not the whole of it. UI is what's on screen: buttons, content, images,
their arrangement — the bridge between the user and the system.

Everything else that UX covers includes:

- Cognitive science — what people want and need, why they react the way they do
- Competitive research
- Business needs, business goals, the product's reason for being

> **Christine Crumlish (Yahoo!):** it's not cosmetics, not pixel pushing, not button placement. It's
> holistic — everyone's concern, not just the realm of artistic types.

UXD isn't practiced only by designers. Project managers, developers, database designers, and software
architects all share responsibility, because the **sum total** of everyone's actions is what creates
the experience.

### Myth 2 — UXD is a step in a process

It *is* the process — from inception (what do we want to build, why, for whom) through to the shipped
website, app, or device.

> **Liz Danzico (School of Visual Arts):** UXD isn't a checkbox. You don't do it and move on.

**Form follows function is only partly true.** In practice, *every force evolves form*: client demands,
budget, time limits, staffing limits, audience preconceptions, internal politics — all of it shapes the
final product and the quality of the experience.

> **Dan Brown (EightShapes):** people expect UXD to be a discrete activity, but it must be an ongoing
> effort — continually learning about users, responding to behaviors, evolving the product or service.

### Myth 3 — UXD is only about technology

All product design considers user experience. A kitchen faucet's handle is shaped so it grips well and
moves easily; a tap-on/tap-off faucet is a different experience decision.

> **Bill DeRouchey:** UXD is not limited to the confines of the computer. It doesn't even need a screen.

> **Mario Bourque (Trapeze Group):** it's about how we live, about everything we do. It surrounds us.

Experience is also about **context** — how you use one thing alongside everything else you use.

### Myth 4 — UXD is just usability

Usability is the science of efficiency and effectiveness: a functional, scientific view of how things
work. Optimize only for that and you get **the Flowbee** — a Shop-Vac with a cutting blade for cutting
your own hair. It was functional; nobody wanted to feel like a fool using it. It was embarrassing and
uncomfortable, and it vanished.

> **Chris Fahey (Behavior):** people think UXD is a way to turn products that suck into products that
> don't, just by throwing resources at design. Perfect form + function + usability still doesn't mean
> anyone will want it.

> **David Malouf (SCAD):** a focus on efficiency and effectiveness blurs other important factors —
> learnability, and visceral/behavioral emotional response.

Everything we use provokes an emotional response. Is it comfortable? Enjoyable? Frustrating?
Aggravating? Confusing? Those responses decide whether something actually gets used.

**Takeaway: usability alone does not make a successful product.**

### Myth 5 — UXD is only about users

It's about the **business** too — the organization whose stability and financial growth depend on the
product.

**The value loop:** value goes out to users → they have a good experience and get value from the
product → value returns to the organization as money made, money saved, or increased use.

> **Russ Unger:** there are business objectives that need to be met, and we're designing to those as
> well. We can't always just do what's best for users.

> **Whitney Hess:** find the sweet spot between user needs and business goals, and ensure the design is
> **on brand**.

*On brand* means the product accurately represents the organization behind it. If the brand says
"innovative, easy, simple" but people spend 20 minutes figuring out how to turn it on, you're off brand.

## 3. What UXD *Is*

A broad term covering **all aspects** of a person's experience with something — an app, a website, a
handheld product, even the drive to work. It's the interfaces you touch, the graphics, the industrial
design, the physical interaction, and the cues that tell you what to do, when, and how.

It's also a **practice**: generating predictive, desirable designs by accounting for how humans are
wired, how we interpret things, and what we consider good or bad.

### The formal definition

> **Jesse James Garrett**, *The Elements of User Experience* — the authoritative source, and the person
> who coined the phrase "user experience":
>
> "User experience simply refers to the way a product behaves and is used in the real world. A positive
> user experience is one in which the goals of both the user and the organization that created the
> product are met."
>
> "Usability is one attribute of a successful user experience. But usability alone does not make an
> experience positive for the user."

It's a two-sided equation: user needs met **and** organizational goals met. Not one or the other.

### UX as a simple equation

```
business goals
+ customer goals
+ user interface        (the part a person interacts with)
+ back-end processes    (what enables the interface to do its job)
= user experience
```

This is the makeup of any system — digital on a screen or physical in a car. Break down the parts and
it's really no different.

### The UX Honeycomb (Peter Morville)

For an experience to be positive, it has to be:

| Attribute | |
|---|---|
| **Useful** | it serves a purpose |
| **Desirable** | people want it |
| **Accessible** | people can get to it |
| **Credible** | people trust it |
| **Findable** | people can locate it and what's in it |
| **Usable** | people can operate it |

All of those together make it **valuable**.

### The activities involved

Sometimes one person, sometimes a multi-discipline team — but all of it plays into UXD:

- **User research** — who we're creating for, what they want and expect, what's valuable to them
- **Interaction design** — how they interact, and how we make sure they understand how to
- **Visual design** — what they actually see on screen, table, or dashboard
- **Information architecture** — what content is needed, how it's organized, prioritized, how much
- **Front-end development** — surfacing the complex logic and data that drive the application
- **Writing** — language is crucial; words and images go hand in hand
- **User testing** — validate assumptions by watching real people use it

### UXD means problem solving

> "If you don't come up with a good solution to something, you probably don't have a very good problem."

Design is about identifying the *right* problems to solve — the ones with the most impact and the most
measurable value — and exploring **feasible** solutions. Anything involving human beings is inherently
messy, so these are hard problems.

We uncover user needs and business goals, and **the overlap between them is where competitive advantage
lives**. Those intersections are what you seek out.

Done well, it means products meet or exceed expectations → increased adoption and use → more loyalty →
and, as a fringe benefit, fewer support calls, meaning efficiency and cost savings.

### Why people actually use things

- They can easily figure out how to accomplish a task
- The steps *seem* simple, even when the task is genuinely complex (the three-second gut check says
  "yeah, I can do that")
- **Effort and cognitive load are minimized** — cognitive load being how much information you must
  absorb and process to do something. Less effort → more likely to start, and more likely to succeed
- **Immediate, clear feedback** for every action. Without it we get anxious, then frustrated, then we
  stop
- **Error potential is minimized**, and when we do slip, we recover quickly and easily

### The balancing act

At its core sits **innovation** — either a genuinely new idea, or the re-imagining of something that
already exists to make it better. Innovation hangs you out on the edge of a cliff: it lives or dies on
a bold idea. It must be balanced against:

- **Desirability** — does anybody want it? Will people make an emotional or intellectual connection?
- **Feasibility** — can we actually design, resource, and build it?
- **Viability** — does it have legs? Will it be adopted, grow, survive several versions?

Balancing all these competing needs is not easy — which is exactly why applying UXD consistently and
correctly matters.

## 4. Why Should We Care About UXD?

### 1. Our creations are used by people

Unless you're making art for your own wall, what you build is meant to be used. Everyone has
experiences, good and bad — and **the bad ones stick with you**. They feel like they last forever, so
you avoid repeating them at all costs.

### 2. Bad experiences get broadcast

In the age of social networks, the first thing we do after a bad experience is tell everyone. Others
then avoid it too. You don't want to be the thing people complain about online.

**Don't be the used-car salesman.** He blocks your progress by refusing to give straight answers, he's
mildly offensive, and he keeps showing you things you explicitly said you didn't want. You walk away
saying "never again." Nobody wants the online equivalent of that guy.

### 3. What people actually want

- **Value** — we're pressed for time, energy, and money, so we need our money's worth
- **Our needs met** — or at least a clear signal that what's inside *could* meet them. That's
  communication: sending the right message
- **Our questions answered** — we arrive with built-in questions: What is this? Is it what I'm after?
  Is it worth more of my time? What if I get stuck? How do I talk to a human?
- **Respect** — respect our time by giving us what we came for, or at least addressing it. Even "we
  don't do that here, but here are three things that might help" is real value

### 4. The old rules of customer service still apply

During the dot-com boom around 2000, the media insisted the old rules of business were out the window.
They weren't true then and aren't now. We still want to be guided, led to what we seek, and given the
sense that someone cares enough to return value for our time and money.

**Walmart example:** boasting about having 16 cash registers means nothing when only two are staffed.
Talking the talk isn't enough — you have to walk the walk. Good customer service and good UXD are the
same thing.

### 5. Expectations are formed visually, first

Your first interaction on screen produces an instant opinion.

**The Nest thermostat** looks simple, modern, technologically advanced, and pleasing — partly because
there's so little visual information. And the device delivers on that impression: instead of
programming "Tuesday 2pm this temperature, Tuesday 5pm ten degrees lower," you just set the temperature
you want when you want it, and it learns your habits without being told. Very little effort, very large
return — value for your effort.

We make instant assumptions in seconds about what a thing does, how well it does it, and what it will
cost us — not only in money but in **effort**. We act, or don't, on those first impressions.

### 6. Cross-channel expectations are higher

A channel is just a delivery method: desktop, laptop, phone, tablet. We expect the same thing to behave
the same way across all of them — a bank website should work as well on a phone as on a laptop.

A Harris Interactive study found most people want better experience design and care less about the brand
itself. A lifelong Apple user starts looking elsewhere the moment Apple disappoints (the instructor
bought a $199 Google Nexus 7 after Apple's software missteps). **People care more about getting things
done efficiently, effectively, and painlessly than about your brand** — they'll bail in an instant.

### 7. My device, my way

Context determines the device. Standing in line at the grocery store checking a price or a review →
phone. At home with a bigger screen available → laptop or tablet. Good UXD keeps the experience
consistent across all those pieces.

### 8. Task completion is not the same as success

Just because someone got from point A to point B doesn't mean they succeeded. If it wasn't easy getting
there, they won't be back. They may have done it because they *had* to. If your model depends on repeat
use, that path has to be painless, elegant, and simple.

### 9. It makes things enjoyable

You could buy the sensible economy car that gets 58 mpg — small, square, not much fun to drive. Or the
convertible. That's the difference between great UXD and plain usability/functionality: **UXD satisfies
the emotional need alongside the functional, tactical one.**

## Section takeaways

1. UX is an elephant — don't describe the whole from the one part you happened to touch.
2. UXD ≠ UI design, ≠ a process step, ≠ technology only, ≠ usability, ≠ users only.
3. A positive experience meets **both** user needs and business goals — the value loop.
4. Useful, desirable, accessible, credible, findable, usable → **valuable**.
5. UXD is problem solving; the overlap of user needs and business goals is where advantage lives.
6. Balance innovation against desirability, feasibility, and viability.
7. Usability gets the task done; UXD makes people want to come back.
